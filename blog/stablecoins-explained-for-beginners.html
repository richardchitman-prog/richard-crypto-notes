Stablecoins, Explained Like You've Never Touched Crypto

October 04, 2026

When I first started reading about crypto, stablecoins were the thing nobody bothered to explain. Everyone just assumed you already knew. So if you've seen "USDT" or "USDC" somewhere and quietly wondered what makes them different from Bitcoin, this one's for you.

The basic idea

Most cryptocurrencies move around a lot in price. That's kind of the whole story with Bitcoin or Ethereum — up a lot, down a lot, sometimes in the same afternoon. A stablecoin is built to do the opposite. It's a type of crypto that's designed to hold a steady value, usually pegged to something like the US dollar. One stablecoin is supposed to be worth close to one dollar, today, tomorrow, and next year.

That's the whole pitch: all the technical plumbing of crypto, none of the rollercoaster.

Why they exist at all

I used to wonder why you'd bother with a "crypto dollar" instead of just using actual dollars. The answer, once I sat with it, made sense. If you're moving money between exchanges, or holding value between trades without converting back to a bank account, you need something that doesn't swing in value while you wait. Stablecoins fill that gap. They let you step out of volatile assets without fully leaving the crypto system.

They've also become a kind of common unit of account across different platforms — a shared language that isn't tied to any single country's banking hours or borders.

Not all stablecoins are built the same way

This is the part that took me longer to understand, and it matters:

  • Fiat-backed: Some stablecoins are backed by actual dollars (or similar assets) sitting in reserve somewhere. The idea is that for every coin issued, there's a dollar held to back it. USDC and USDT fall roughly into this category, though how transparently and how fully they're backed has been a real point of scrutiny over the years.
  • Crypto-backed: Others are backed by other cryptocurrencies, over-collateralized to absorb price swings. More complex, more moving parts.
  • Algorithmic: A few try to hold their peg through code and incentives rather than reserves. These have had the roughest track record — there are well-known examples of algorithmic stablecoins collapsing entirely.

The label "stable" is doing a lot of work in that last category. It's worth remembering a peg is a design goal, not a guarantee.

What I keep in mind now

Stability is relative. "Stable" compared to Bitcoin isn't the same as "stable" compared to money in an insured bank account. A stablecoin depends on trust in whoever issues it and whatever backs it. That's a different kind of risk than price swings, but it's still risk, and it's worth understanding before you treat any stablecoin as a dollar with extra steps.

I'm not writing this to tell you to use one or avoid one. I just didn't want to keep nodding along in conversations without actually knowing what the term meant.
