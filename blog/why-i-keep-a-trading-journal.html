Why I Keep a Trading Journal (and What Actually Goes In It)

September 27, 2026

I didn't start keeping a trading journal because someone told me to. I started because I kept making the same mistake three different ways and couldn't figure out why. Writing things down turned out to be the cheapest tool I own — no cost, no subscription, just a notebook (well, a Google Doc, but same idea).

Here's what that habit actually looks like day to day, not the polished version.

What I write before I take a trade

  • Why I'm considering it — the actual reason, not the feeling. "Price bounced off a level I've watched before" is different from "I have a hunch."
  • What the chart looked like at the moment I decided, usually a screenshot with a line drawn where I think support or resistance is.
  • What would prove me wrong. This one took me the longest to start doing consistently, and it's probably the most useful line in the whole entry.

What I write after

This is the part most people skip, and it's the part that actually teaches you something. I write down what happened, sure, but more importantly I write down how I felt about it. Did I exit early because I was nervous, or because the setup actually failed? Did I hold too long hoping it would turn around? The outcome of the trade is data. My reaction to the outcome is a pattern, and patterns are what you can actually fix.

I also log the boring stuff — date, size, entry, exit, result. Not because I'm trying to build a highlight reel, but because a few months in, the boring stuff is what shows you the real story. Anyone can remember their best trade. Almost nobody accurately remembers their average one.

What it's not

It's not a brag sheet. I don't post my entries anywhere, and honestly most of them are unremarkable — small trades, small lessons, occasional "I have no idea why I did that." It's also not a replacement for actually learning how to read a chart in the first place. A journal just tells you the truth about decisions you already know how to make. If the decisions themselves are shaky, no amount of journaling fixes that — that part came from slower, more deliberate study, plus a fair number of free resources I worked through early on, including the boot camp material I linked below when I was first trying to make sense of candlesticks and levels.

The pattern that surprised me

The single biggest thing my journal exposed wasn't a chart pattern — it was a time-of-day pattern. I lose more often on trades I take in the last twenty minutes before I have to log off for something else. Rushed decisions, apparently, look a lot like bad decisions. I never would have caught that without months of entries to look back over. No indicator was going to tell me that. Only the log could.

Why bother

I'll be honest — it's a little tedious some days, especially after a trade that didn't need much explaining. But the entries I resent writing the most are usually the ones I learn the most from.
